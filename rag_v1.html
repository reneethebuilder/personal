Step 2

Chunks → embeddings → vectors

A chunk is text. An embedding is a numerical representation of that text. This simulator hashes semantic concepts into a small vector so you can inspect the idea without an API.

Chunk ≠ vector

Chunk: human-readable text. Vector: coordinates used for similarity search. Real embedding models usually produce hundreds or thousands of dimensions.

Why cosine?

Cosine similarity compares the angle between vectors, reducing sensitivity to their magnitude. A higher cosine similarity generally means more similar direction.

Metadata still matters

Vectors do not replace source, date, permissions, product, region or document type. Metadata filters can prevent irrelevant—or unauthorized—retrieval.

Failure lab

What can go wrong in RAG?

A weak answer can come from bad data, bad retrieval, bad prompt construction, or generation. Debug the stage instead of assuming “the LLM is bad.”

1. Stale or contradictory sources

Old and new policies can both rank highly. Add freshness metadata, effective dates, versioning and source-of-truth rules.

2. OCR / parsing failures

Scanned PDFs, tables, headers and multi-column layouts may become garbled. Retrieval cannot find facts that ingestion lost.

3. Bad chunk boundaries

Too small: facts lose context. Too large: unrelated topics dilute the embedding and consume prompt budget. Overlap can help, but too much creates duplicates.

4. Embedding mismatch

A model may not represent your domain vocabulary, language, code, formulas or acronyms well. Evaluate retrieval on real queries.

5. Dense-only blind spots

Semantic search can miss exact identifiers, SKUs, legal clauses or error codes. Sparse/BM25-style retrieval often complements dense retrieval.

6. Sparse-only blind spots

Exact-term search misses paraphrases and conceptually similar language. Hybrid search can improve recall.

7. Top K too low

The needed passage may never reach the model. Retrieval recall drops.

8. Top K too high

Noise, contradictions, latency, cost and “lost in the middle” effects increase. More context is not automatically better context.

9. Threshold too strict

You may retrieve nothing even when a usable result exists. Thresholds should be calibrated against labeled queries.

10. No reranking

Fast first-stage retrieval may have good recall but imperfect ordering. A reranker can spend more compute on a small candidate set.

11. Missing metadata filters

A globally similar chunk may be wrong for the user's region, product, role, date or account. Filter before or during retrieval.

12. Permission leakage

Access control must be enforced at retrieval time. Do not retrieve a document merely because the vector is similar.

13. Prompt injection in retrieved text

Documents are untrusted data. Retrieved instructions should not silently override system or developer rules.

14. Context packing problems

Duplicate chunks, poor ordering and oversized passages waste tokens. Deduplicate, diversify and preserve useful source labels.

15. Hallucination despite good retrieval

A model can still invent or overgeneralize. Require grounded answers, citations, abstention when evidence is insufficient, and factual evaluation.

16. Evaluating only the final answer

Separate retrieval metrics from generation metrics. Otherwise you cannot tell whether the retriever or generator caused the failure.

Recall@K

Of all relevant passages, how many did the retriever return in the first K results? High recall is especially important before reranking.

Precision@K

Of the K retrieved passages, how many are actually relevant? Higher precision means less noise enters the prompt.

End-to-end groundedness

Does the answer make claims supported by retrieved evidence? This is different from whether the answer merely sounds plausible.
